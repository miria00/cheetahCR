\section{Limitations, Future Work, and Broader Impact}\label{app:limitations}
CHEETAH is a new mathematical abstraction for global HW/SW co-design. It
captures exact cross-stage trade-offs that sequential pipelines cannot express,
and a rounded, simulated replay shows that these trade-offs are not an LP
artifact. The decomposition of the speedups in Figure~\ref{fig:speedup}, up to
$24.20\times$ on LLM prefill and $17.93\times$ on LLM decode, and the sensitivity
sweeps show that the gain is not merely the result of adding MACs. This
manuscript therefore gives a formal and empirical demonstration that sequential
tiling-then-fusion loses solutions, together with a joint optimization method
that recovers those solutions in precisely characterized operating regimes.
Sequential accelerator pipelines make locally optimal mapping decisions before
they know the global memory schedule, whereas CHEETAH exposes tile choice,
tensor residency, and rematerialization to one optimization problem. We prove
that the lifted sequential feasible set is contained in CHEETAH's, isolate a
fixed-hardware free-$y$ gain on Llama-3 70B, and demonstrate a second strict
separation in capacity-constrained DeepSeek-V3, where tile-dependent footprints
enable schedules unavailable to both the stock pipeline and an oracle two-pass
sequential baseline.

The reported efficiency quantities are roofline-model diagnostics, not
measured silicon utilization. Real implementations would still incur NoC,
SRAM, pipeline, and control overheads. Future work can build on this framework
in two meaningful ways: as an end-to-end co-design system for efficient
accelerators, and as an automated optimization engine for existing hardware.
By fixing the architecture, the inner loop derives optimal tiling and
scheduling strategies that act as custom speedups for targeted inference tasks.
The moment for this work is unusually consequential. Inference demand is
growing far faster than general-purpose silicon improves, and the data centers
built to meet it now draw power on a scale that shapes grid planning and
energy policy. Every point of utilization recovered by a better schedule, and
every watt removed by a chip sized to its workload, is multiplied across
billions of daily requests. The same formulation serves users who will never
design a chip. A research group with one fixed GPU node, a startup serving a
single model, or a public institution running inference under a hard power
budget can each ask the inner optimizer for the best schedule its hardware
admits, with a certificate of how far from the model ceiling it sits. For
research, an exact and auditable co-design program opens questions that
heuristic pipelines could not pose: which architectural features a model
family provably needs, how much of a speedup is physics and how much is
scheduling, and what a new network should look like if it is to run well on
the chips that exist. Three directions are most urgent. The declared model
should extend to training and to multi-chip systems. The loop should close
with fabricated silicon, so that the cost model is calibrated rather than
assumed. The solver should become fast enough to sit inside a compiler.
Together these would turn hardware/software co-design from a sequence of
expert judgments into a reproducible optimization that any team can run, at a
time when the cost of getting it wrong is measured in gigawatts.
