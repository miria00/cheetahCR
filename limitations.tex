\section{Limitations, Future Work, and Broader Impact}\label{app:limitations}
CHEETAH is a new mathematical abstraction for global HW/SW co-design. It
exposes tile choice, tensor residency, rematerialization, fusion and hardware
provisioning to one linear program, so cross-stage trade-offs that sequential
pipelines cannot express become ordinary variables of a single optimization.
Sequential accelerator pipelines make locally optimal mapping decisions before
they know the global memory schedule, and we prove that the lifted sequential
feasible set is contained in CHEETAH's, isolate a fixed-hardware free-$y$ gain
on Llama-3 70B, and demonstrate a second strict separation in
capacity-constrained DeepSeek-V3, where tile-dependent footprints enable
schedules unavailable to both the stock pipeline and an oracle two-pass
sequential baseline. The decomposition of the speedups in
Figure~\ref{fig:speedup}, up to $24.20\times$ on LLM prefill and $17.93\times$
on LLM decode, and the sensitivity sweeps show that the gain is not merely the
result of adding MACs, and a rounded, simulated replay shows that the
trade-offs are not an LP artifact. This is the first formal and empirical
demonstration that tiling-then-fusion loses solutions, paired with a joint
optimization method that recovers them in precisely characterized operating
regimes, with a certificate that says how far any schedule can still go. It
matters because inference demand is growing far faster than general-purpose
silicon improves, and every point of utilization recovered by a better
schedule, and every watt removed by a chip sized to its workload, is multiplied
across billions of daily requests.

The reported efficiency quantities are roofline-model diagnostics, not
measured silicon utilization, and real implementations would still incur NoC,
SRAM, pipeline and control overheads. The certificate is relative to the
declared implementation library and cost model, and the RTL evidence validates
mechanisms rather than a fabricated chip. These limits bound what the numbers
claim, not the formulation.

Future work can build on this framework in two meaningful ways: as an
end-to-end co-design system for efficient accelerators, and as an automated
optimization engine for existing hardware, where the inner loop derives
optimal tiling and scheduling strategies that act as custom speedups for
targeted inference tasks. The same formulation serves users who will never
design a chip. A research group with one fixed GPU node, a startup serving a
single model, or a public institution running inference under a hard power
budget can each ask the inner optimizer for the best schedule its hardware
admits, with a certificate of how far from the model ceiling it sits. For
research, an exact and auditable co-design program opens questions that
heuristic pipelines could not pose: which architectural features a model
family provably needs, how much of a speedup is physics and how much is
scheduling, and what a new network should look like if it is to run well on
the chips that exist. Three directions are most urgent. The declared model
should extend to training and to multi-chip systems, so that a whole cluster
and its parallelism strategy become one program. The loop should close with
fabricated silicon, so that the cost model is calibrated rather than assumed
and each tape-out tightens the next certificate. The solver should become fast
enough to sit inside a compiler, so that every deployment is scheduled to a
certified gap at build time. Together these would turn hardware/software
co-design from a sequence of expert judgments into a reproducible optimization
that any team can run, at a time when the cost of getting it wrong is measured
in gigawatts.
